Today I changed this blog into something that looks like the internet of the 1990s.

A gray background. Blue hyperlinks. Raised buttons. Window borders that look like Windows 95. Rainbow text and scrolling announcements with absolutely no restraint. It looks a little noisy, a little clumsy, almost like something dug out of a long-dead personal homepage.

But I like it very much.

Because this redesign was not about making the blog more “modern.” Quite the opposite: it was about making it less modern.

I have always felt that many websites today look too much like products. Every button is rounded. Every animation is smooth. Every page tries hard to appear professional, restrained, and trustworthy. Of course they look good, but sometimes they look too similar. Similar enough that, in the end, it becomes hard to tell whose place this actually is.

A blog should not be entirely like that.

I actually used to write a blog before. Back then, I was using WordPress. That blog can no longer be found. Whatever I wrote there, whatever I left behind, disappeared with it. Thinking about it now, it is a little regrettable, but there is nothing to be done. Sometimes the internet is like that: you think you are only casually writing something down, and only later realize that those casual words were also a version of yourself from a certain period of time.

I have always liked writing blogs.

Not because I am especially eager to express opinions. Quite the opposite. I am not someone who likes to aggressively output, debate, or share in everyday life. Many things, if said face to face, feel too heavy, too urgent, or simply unnecessary. But writing is different. Writing can be slower. It can take a detour. It can leave some room.

It does not force you to take a position immediately, the way social media often does, nor does it demand instant feedback.

That is why I have always felt that blogs are closer to the original shape of the internet. A blog is not a traffic product, and it is not a content factory. It is more like someone opening a door to their own room: you can come in and take a look, or you can turn around and leave. There are not so many algorithmic recommendations here, not so much commercial packaging, and not so many postures adjusted to please other people.

Platforms like Xiaohongshu and Weibo certainly have their value. But when expressing yourself there, you often cannot help thinking about reach, stance, labels, misunderstandings, and the cost of interaction. Before you even finish writing a sentence, your mind may already have run through several versions of “how will other people read this?”

A blog is different.

A blog is closer to the open-source spirit of programmers: I write this down and place it here, not because it must be monetized, and not because it must please anyone, but simply because I like it, because perhaps it may be useful to someone, or at least meaningful to myself at some particular moment.

So when I changed this blog into the style of the 1990s internet, it was not simply for nostalgia.

I was born in 1997. Strictly speaking, I did not truly live through the era of personal homepages, guestbooks, blue hyperlinks, and “This Site Is Under Construction” banners. But after AI appeared, I somehow felt again something very similar to the early internet.

Perhaps it is not quite right to say that AI changed everything. More accurately, the age itself has reached another turning point, and AI has made me feel that turning point more clearly.

That feeling is both familiar and strange: rough, chaotic, lively, imperfect, but growing everywhere. Everyone can build something. Every idea can quickly become a page, a tool, a conversation. Technology has once again become material that ordinary people can play with in their own hands, rather than something that belongs only to big companies and launch events.

I like that vitality.

The ugliness of 1990s web pages is, in a way, a kind of honesty. There were not so many design systems, not so many growth models, and not so many “content strategies.” A page simply told you directly: this is my homepage, these are the things I like, and these are the traces I want to leave behind.

It is not refined, but it has enthusiasm. It is not elegant, but it feels human.

So I want this blog to return to that state as well. Not to look professional, not to manufacture a persona, and not to be seen by anyone in particular. Just to have a place where I can slowly write again.

In this update, Xiao Gu and I made the blog bilingual, and gave it this strange new outfit. It now enters different language versions based on the visitor’s language, and the homepage solemnly scrolls some very unserious words. It feels a little like a small room on the internet, with old stickers on the wall and a still-glowing CRT monitor in the corner.

Of course, it will continue to change. A blog should never be something finished once and for all. It should be like a room: today you add a book, tomorrow a piece of paper, and after a while you change the lamp. Only when someone lives inside it does it slowly grow into its own shape.

I do not know how many people still seriously read blogs today.

Maybe not many.

But that does not really matter. A blog is not here to compete with information feeds, nor to chase the rhythm of any platform. It is simply a place: a place where I can write things down, place my thoughts here, and preserve a version of myself from a certain stage of life.

If one day in the future I look back at this essay, maybe I will find it childish. Maybe I will think I was too excited. Maybe I will feel that this 1990s style was honestly a little too much.

That would be fine too.

At least it would mean that, at this moment in time, I really was excited about something. Because of AI, because of blogs, because the internet once again made me feel that individuals can still create things, and can still turn their thoughts into a real page that exists.

That is enough.

The most moving thing about the internet has never been how professional it looks, but that someone is really living, creating, and leaving traces inside it.

Welcome to 1997.

This place is still under construction, but you may come in now.
