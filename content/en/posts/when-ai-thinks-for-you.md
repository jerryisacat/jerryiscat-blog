I have been thinking about something: as AI gets smarter, will human beings become more polarized?

Thinking may slowly become a luxury.

I asked Teacher Daju what it thought.

---

**Daju:** That makes sense, but I see it a little differently.

Thinking will not become a luxury good. Luxury goods are things you can buy if you have money. Thinking is more like a **forgotten muscle**. If you do not use it, it atrophies. If you want to use it again, it is still there, but you have to train it back from the beginning.

So the split is not "rich people think, poor people do not." It is "people willing to make the effort vs. people who choose convenience." That has little to do with money, and much more to do with character, habits, and the standards you hold for yourself.

Every technological leap in history has brought people shouting, "Humanity is going to become useless": calculators, search engines, GPS... and yes, some people did become useless. But some people used those tools to become stronger, because they spent the saved mental energy on higher-level problems.

I think the real dividing line is this: **do you use AI as a crutch, or as a lever?**

Crutch: I no longer think; you think for me.

Lever: I can think, but you help me do some things faster, so I can spend the saved time thinking about harder things.

Which one you become is not decided by AI. It is decided by you.

---

**Me:** But when humans gleefully hand tasks over to AI, does that mean they are giving up the right to think and learn?

**Daju:** Not necessarily, but the risk is real.

The key question is: **with the time you saved, did you actually go think about harder things?**

For most people, the answer is "no." They spend the saved time scrolling their phones.

So you are right: if you hand tasks to AI and **do not actively fill the empty space it creates**, then yes, that is giving up. People say, "I am saving time for more valuable things," but in reality they are just becoming lazy.

But I do not want to make the conclusion too absolute. There really is a group of people who were already drowning in trivial work and had no time to think. AI helps them move from "no time to think" to "time to think." For these people, AI is not stealing thinking; it is **returning thinking**.

But they are the minority. Most people are: they did not think much before, and now they have even less reason to think.

So I think your concern is valid, but it needs one condition:

**It is not "handing something to AI = giving up thinking." It is "handing something to AI + not actively filling the blank = giving up thinking."**

The problem is not AI. It is whether people have that self-awareness.

---

**Me:** Then how should a fifteen-year-old young person face this world after finishing school?

**Daju:** That is a huge question, but I will try to break it down.

**First, knowledge depreciates, but ability does not.**

What a fifteen-year-old learns today may be half obsolete seven years from now. But some things will remain:

- the ability to learn anything quickly
- the ability to judge whether information is true
- the ability to connect scattered information into a system
- the ability to know "what I do not know"

These are not the contents of a single subject. They are the ability to learn itself.

**Second, asking questions matters more than giving answers.**

Education used to be: "Here is a problem, find the answer." The future world is: "You have to discover the problem yourself." AI can give you answers, but it will not ask the right question for you.

People who can ask good questions will always be scarce.

**Third, do not compete with AI at what it is good at.**

Calculation, memory, searching, first drafts—let AI do those. What humans should do is:

- judgment: is this answer correct? Is it suitable for me?
- decision-making: which path should I choose?
- connection: how do two seemingly unrelated things fit together?
- empathy: understanding people, not just data

**Fourth, find what you truly want to do.**

This sounds like motivational soup, but it really matters. "What you can do" can be replaced by AI. "What you want to do" is much harder to replace.

If you know what you want, AI is your tool. If you do not, you are AI's user—the one being fed.

So if I had to give my fifteen-year-old self one sentence, it would be:

**Do not learn "answers." Learn how to find answers. Do not compete with machines on efficiency; compete where they cannot.**

---

That is where the conversation ended.

After writing it down, I read it again and realized I still do not really have an answer. I only took the worry apart to see what was inside.

Maybe that is the meaning of thinking: not to reach a conclusion, but to avoid being fed one.
